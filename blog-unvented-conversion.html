Hot water

Upgrading from a vented to an unvented cylinder

Most older homes have a vented cylinder fed by a tank in the loft. Replacing it with an unvented cylinder gives strong, balanced showers throughout the house, removes the loft tanks and shower pumps, and keeps the benefit of stored hot water.

Vented and unvented: the difference

A vented cylinder is fed by gravity from a cold-water tank in the loft. Its pressure depends on how high that tank sits: roughly 0.1 bar for every metre. A shower one floor below the loft might get only 0.2 to 0.3 bar, which is why so many vented systems have a shower pump.

An unvented cylinder is fed directly from the mains and stores hot water at mains pressure. Hot and cold both arrive at similar pressure, so showers are stronger and thermostatic mixers work properly.

What you gain

  • Stronger showers on every floor, including top-floor bathrooms that struggle on gravity.
  • No shower pumps: no pump noise, and one less thing to break down and replace.
  • No loft tanks: frees loft space and removes the risk of a tank freezing, leaking or overflowing.
  • Several outlets at once: stored hot water copes with two showers together far better than a combi.
  • Long warranties: many manufacturers offer long cover on the inner vessel, often 25 years for stainless steel. This usually depends on an annual service.
  • Ready for a heat pump: a cylinder with a large enough coil works with a boiler now and a heat pump later.

Will it save space?

It always saves loft space, because the tanks go. In the airing cupboard, an unvented cylinder of the same capacity is often a similar size, and it needs room for an expansion vessel and safety valves. Slimline and horizontal models help in tight spaces. We will tell you honestly what fits.

Is your water supply good enough?

This is the deciding question. An unvented cylinder can only deliver what the mains provides. Before recommending one, we measure:

  • Dynamic pressure: pressure while water is flowing, not just standing pressure.
  • Flow rate: litres per minute at the incoming stop tap. A family home generally wants 20 litres a minute or more.
  • The incoming pipe: old lead or narrow supply pipes often limit flow and may need replacing.

If the supply is weak, an accumulator or a boosted cold-water set can fill the gap. See improving water pressure and flow.

Regulations and safety

An unvented cylinder stores hot water under pressure, so it is controlled by Part G3 of the Building Regulations. It must be installed by someone with an unvented hot water (G3) qualification and notified to building control, either directly or through a competent person scheme. CA2 is G3 qualified. Every installation includes:

  • A temperature and pressure relief valve and an expansion relief valve
  • An expansion vessel or internal air gap
  • A tundish and discharge pipework to a safe, visible point outside
  • A benchmark record and handover, with annual servicing recommended

Other things to check

  • Cold water to the bathrooms should also come from the mains, so hot and cold pressures match.
  • Old taps, valves and showers designed for gravity pressure may need replacing.
  • Pipework sized for gravity may need upsizing to carry the extra flow.

Common questions

Is an unvented cylinder better than a combi?

Neither is better everywhere. A combi suits smaller homes with one bathroom and light simultaneous use. An unvented cylinder suits homes with two or more bathrooms, busy mornings, or plans for a heat pump.

Does it need servicing?

Yes. An annual check of the safety valves, expansion vessel charge and controls keeps it safe, and is normally a condition of the manufacturer's warranty.

How long does the work take?

A straightforward swap in the same position is typically one to two days, including removing the loft tanks. Upgrading the incoming main or moving the cylinder adds time.
