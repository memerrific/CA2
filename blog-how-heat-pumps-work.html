How it works

How a heat pump works

A heat pump doesn't burn anything. It moves heat that is already in the outdoor air into your heating and hot water, using electricity to do the moving. Done well, it delivers around three units of heat or more for every unit of electricity it uses.

The refrigeration cycle, in four steps

A heat pump works like a fridge in reverse. A refrigerant circulates in a sealed loop and does four things, over and over:

  1. Evaporate: outdoor air is blown across a coil containing very cold refrigerant. Even on a freezing day, the air is warmer than the refrigerant, so it absorbs heat and boils into a gas.
  2. Compress: the compressor squeezes that gas, which raises its temperature sharply. This is where the electricity goes.
  3. Condense: the hot gas passes through a heat exchanger and gives its heat to the water in your heating system, turning back into a liquid.
  4. Expand: an expansion valve drops the pressure, the refrigerant becomes very cold again, and the cycle repeats.

What COP means

Efficiency is measured as the coefficient of performance (COP): heat out divided by electricity in. A COP of 3 means 3 kWh of heat for every 1 kWh of electricity. COP changes with the weather and the water temperature, so the figure to look at is the seasonal average across a whole year, often called SCOP or SPF.

Why flow temperature matters most

The harder a heat pump has to lift the temperature, from outdoor air up to your radiator water, the less efficient it is. Running radiators at 35 to 45 °C instead of 60 to 70 °C is what makes a heat pump cheap to run. That is why the design work matters more than the brand:

  • A room-by-room heat-loss calculation shows what each room needs
  • Radiators sized to deliver it at a low temperature
  • Pipework sized for the larger flow a heat pump needs
  • Weather compensation so the water is only as warm as the day requires

Things that surprise people

  • It runs for longer, more gently. Radiators feel warm rather than hot, and the house stays at a steady temperature. That is normal and efficient.
  • It defrosts itself. In cold, damp weather, ice forms on the outdoor coil. The unit briefly reverses to melt it, sometimes with a puff of steam.
  • Hot water is stored. A cylinder is heated to around 50 °C, with a regular higher-temperature cycle for hygiene.
  • Placement matters. The outdoor unit needs free airflow, a sound base and sensible distance from neighbours. Planning rules on siting and noise apply, and we check them at survey.

Refrigerants

Most current units use R32 or R290 (propane). R290 has a very low environmental impact and can reach higher water temperatures, which helps homes with smaller radiators. Both are sealed in the outdoor unit at the factory on monobloc models, so there is no refrigerant pipework inside your home.

Common questions

Does a heat pump work in winter?

Yes. Air source heat pumps extract heat from outdoor air well below freezing. Efficiency falls in very cold weather, which the design allows for.

Is it noisy?

Modern units are quiet, typically a low hum similar to a fridge or the fans of an air conditioner. Placement and a solid base make the biggest difference.

Wondering if a heat pump would suit your home?

We start with the heat loss, so the answer is based on your house rather than a guess.
