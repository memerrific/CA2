Heating design

Pipe sizing: why it matters for heat pumps

Pipework is the part of a heating system nobody sees, and it is often what limits a heat pump. Heat pumps move heat with a lot of water at a small temperature difference, so pipes that were fine for a boiler can be too small.

Heat is flow times temperature difference

The heat a pipe carries depends on two things: how much water flows through it, and how much that water cools on its way round. The relationship is simple:

Heat (kW) = flow (litres per second) × 4.18 × temperature drop (°C)

A boiler system typically runs with a 20 °C drop between flow and return. A heat pump is designed for around 5 °C. To carry the same heat with a quarter of the temperature drop, a heat pump needs about four times as much water moving through the pipes.

Why you can't just pump harder

Pushing more water through a small pipe makes it flow faster. Faster water causes noise, wears fittings and needs much more pump pressure, because resistance rises steeply with speed. Designers keep water speed modest, typically below about 1 metre per second in domestic pipework, and size the pipe to suit.

What a pipe can roughly carry

As a guide, at a moderate water speed of about 0.7 metres per second in copper:

Copper pipeApprox. heat at 5 °C drop (heat pump)Approx. heat at 20 °C drop (boiler)
15 mmabout 2 kWabout 8 kW
22 mmabout 4.5 kWabout 18 kW
28 mmabout 8 kWabout 30 kW

These are indicative only. A real design accounts for pipe length, fittings, valves and the pump's available pressure. The point is the scale: a 22 mm main that easily fed a whole house from a boiler may only carry enough heat for part of it at heat-pump temperatures.

What this means for an existing house

  • Primary pipework from the heat pump to the cylinder and the main heating circuit is often upsized, commonly to 28 mm or larger.
  • Branches to individual radiators carry less heat, and 15 mm is often fine. The calculation confirms it.
  • Microbore (8 and 10 mm) can work for small radiators, but long runs to larger radiators often need replacing.
  • Restrictions such as old valves, sludge and undersized fittings matter more when flow is higher. Cleaning and the right valves count.

How we size a system

We start from the room-by-room heat loss, work out the flow each radiator needs, then size each section of pipe so the water speed and pressure loss stay within limits. The pump is chosen from the total, and the system is balanced so each radiator gets its share. See self-balancing radiator valves.

Common questions

Do I have to replace all my pipework for a heat pump?

Usually not. The main circuits and the heat pump connections are most often upsized; many radiator branches can stay. The design shows exactly which sections need changing.

Is plastic pipe a problem?

No, but plastic pipe of the same nominal size has a smaller bore than copper, so it carries a little less. The design uses the actual internal size.
