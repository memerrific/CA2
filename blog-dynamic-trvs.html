Radiators & controls

Self-balancing radiator valves (dynamic TRVs) explained

If some radiators get hot quickly while others stay lukewarm, the system is unbalanced. Dynamic radiator valves fix this automatically by limiting each radiator to exactly the flow it needs.

Why radiators heat unevenly

Water takes the easiest route. Radiators close to the pump get more than their share, and the furthest ones get the leftovers. The traditional fix is to adjust each radiator's lockshield valve by hand. That is slow and fiddly, and it drifts out again every time other valves open and close during the day.

Three kinds of radiator valve

  • Standard TRV: the head contains a wax or liquid sensor that closes the valve as the room warms up. It controls room temperature, not how much water the radiator receives.
  • Pre-settable TRV: the valve body has a dial that caps the maximum flow. Set correctly, each radiator gets its share. The flow still changes, though, when the pressure in the system changes.
  • Dynamic (pressure-independent) TRV: as well as a flow setting, the valve has a built-in regulator that holds that flow steady, whatever the rest of the system is doing. This is the self-regulating valve, and it balances the system automatically. The Danfoss Dynamic Valve is a well-known example.

How the setting is worked out

Each radiator needs a flow that matches the heat it must deliver to its room. That comes from the room-by-room heat loss. The flow is calculated with a simple formula:

Flow (litres per hour) = heat output (W) ÷ (1.163 × temperature drop across the radiator, °C)

A radiator delivering 1,000 W with a 20 °C drop, typical of a boiler, needs about 43 litres an hour. With a 5 °C drop, typical of a heat pump, it needs about 172 litres an hour. Each valve is set to its radiator's figure, so every room gets the heat it was designed for.

Because the setting is a calculated number, not a guess, the result can be checked. When a system is commissioned, flow and return temperatures at each radiator show whether it is getting its share.

Why it matters for heat pumps and condensing boilers

  • Lower return temperatures: a balanced system returns cooler water, so a condensing boiler actually condenses and runs more efficiently.
  • Even heat at low temperatures: heat pumps run at low flow temperatures, so a starved radiator shows up straight away as a cold room.
  • Lower pump setting: when no radiator is hogging flow, the pump can often run at a lower setting.
  • Quieter running: limiting flow at each valve reduces whistling and rushing noises.

Limits worth knowing

  • Dynamic valves need a small minimum pump pressure to regulate, so the pump must be set to suit them.
  • They can't fix undersized pipework or radiators that are simply too small for the room.
  • Leave the room with the main thermostat without a TRV, or fully open, so the two controls don't fight each other.

When they're worth fitting

They are worth considering when replacing radiators, during a heat pump installation, when converting to weather compensation, or on any system with persistent cold rooms. Because the settings come from a heat-loss calculation, we fit them as part of a designed system, not as a swap on its own. See why room-by-room heat loss matters.

Common questions

Can I just swap the TRV heads?

No. The regulating part is in the valve body, so the body has to be changed. Some existing bodies can take a pre-settable insert, which is checked on survey.

Do they work with smart radiator heads?

Many dynamic valve bodies accept common smart and standard heads. Check the thread type before buying heads.

Cold rooms or uneven radiators?

We'll calculate what each radiator needs and balance the system properly.
