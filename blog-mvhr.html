Ventilation

MVHR explained: ventilation, heat recovery and air changes

As homes become better insulated and more airtight, fresh air has to be designed in rather than left to draughts. MVHR supplies fresh air continuously and recovers most of the heat from the stale air it removes.

Why ventilation matters more than it used to

A family produces several litres of water vapour a day from breathing, cooking, showers and drying clothes. In a draughty house, that moisture leaks away along with your heat. Seal the house up and it stays inside, causing condensation, mould and stale air. Good ventilation removes moisture and pollutants without throwing away the heat you have paid for.

How MVHR works

A central unit, usually in the loft or a cupboard, runs two fans continuously:

  • Extract: stale, moist air is drawn from the kitchen, bathrooms and utility room.
  • Supply: fresh outdoor air is delivered to bedrooms and living rooms.
  • Heat recovery: the two airstreams pass each other in a heat exchanger without mixing. Much of the outgoing heat warms the incoming air. Good units recover the large majority of it.

A boost setting raises the airflow for cooking and showers. A summer bypass lets cool night air in without heating it.

Air changes per hour, explained

Air changes per hour (ACH) is how many times the whole volume of air in a home is replaced in an hour:

ACH = airflow (m³ per hour) ÷ volume of the home (m³)

A 100 m² home with 2.4 m ceilings holds 240 m³ of air. A ventilation rate of 120 m³ an hour (about 33 litres a second) replaces it roughly every two hours, which is 0.5 ACH. For comparison, Approved Document F sets the minimum whole-dwelling rate as the higher of a figure based on the number of bedrooms or 0.3 litres a second for every square metre of floor area. For that home, the floor-area figure is 30 litres a second, about 0.45 ACH. The design figure comes from the calculation, not a rule of thumb.

Don't confuse this with an airtightness test result, which is measured at an artificial 50 pascals of pressure. That figure describes how leaky the building is, not how much fresh air it gets.

What good design looks like

  • Calculated airflows for every room, from the ventilation requirement
  • Ductwork sized for low air speed, so the system is quiet; undersized ducts are the most common cause of noise
  • Insulated ducts in cold spaces, so they don't form condensation
  • A condensate drain and frost protection for the unit
  • Commissioning: each valve balanced and airflows measured and recorded at handover

When MVHR makes sense

MVHR works best in airtight homes: new builds, deep retrofits and extensive renovations where ducts can be routed. In a leaky house, much of the air bypasses the heat exchanger, so the recovery benefit drops. For partial upgrades, single-room heat recovery units or continuous extract fans may be a better fit.

Maintenance

Filters need cleaning or replacing roughly every six to twelve months, depending on location. Homes near busy roads or the coast may need more frequent changes. The unit and condensate drain should be checked annually. Neglected filters are the main reason systems become noisy and lose efficiency.

How it links to heating design

Ventilation is part of a home's heat loss. Recovering that heat reduces the size of the heat pump or boiler needed and lowers running costs. That is why we look at ventilation and heating together. See why room-by-room heat loss matters.
