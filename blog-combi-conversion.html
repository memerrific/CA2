Boiler conversions

Converting to a combi boiler: what to expect

A combi heats water as you use it, so there is no cylinder to keep hot and no tanks in the loft. It suits many homes very well. It also behaves differently from the system you have now, and knowing why saves surprises after the work is done.

"Instant" hot water isn't quite instant

The most common comment after a conversion is that hot water takes a little longer to arrive than it used to. There are two reasons, and neither means anything is wrong.

First, the boiler has to notice the tap is open, light, and bring its heat exchanger up to temperature. That usually takes a few seconds.

Second, the cold water already sitting in the pipe between the boiler and the tap has to run out before hot water reaches you. Ten metres of 15 mm copper holds about 1.5 litres. At a typical kitchen-tap flow of 6 litres a minute, that alone is around 15 seconds. Combis are often fitted in the kitchen, a long way from the bathroom, so the pipe run can be longer than it was from your old cylinder.

Many combis have a pre-heat or comfort setting that keeps the heat exchanger warm and cuts the wait. It uses a little extra gas, so it is worth switching on only if the delay bothers you. Routing pipework sensibly and insulating it also helps.

Why the wait is a trade worth making

A cylinder stores 100 litres or more of hot water all day, and that water slowly loses heat whether you use it or not. The boiler then has to reheat it. A combi only heats water at the moment you draw it, so there is no stored hot water cooling down and nothing to reheat. That is where the running-cost saving comes from.

How much hot water a combi can deliver

A combi's hot-water output depends on its size and how much the water has to be heated. A useful rule of thumb:

Flow (litres per minute) ≈ boiler output (kW) × 14.3 ÷ temperature rise (°C)

A 30 kW combi raising water by 35 °C gives roughly 12 litres a minute. In winter, the incoming mains is colder, so the same boiler gives a little less hot water at the same temperature. The incoming mains also has to supply that flow. A big boiler on a weak supply will still give a weak shower, which is why we measure the incoming flow and pressure before recommending one.

One outlet at a time works best

A combi shares its output between every hot tap that is open. One shower is fine. A shower and a kitchen tap at once usually works, with some drop in flow. Two showers at the same time is where combis struggle. If your household regularly runs two showers together, an unvented cylinder is usually the better choice. See upgrading to an unvented cylinder.

What gets removed, and what needs changing

  • Hot water cylinder: removed, usually freeing an airing cupboard.
  • Loft tanks: the cold-water storage tank and the small feed-and-expansion tank can both go. That removes the risk of tanks freezing or overflowing.
  • Shower pumps: these must not be connected to the mains, so they are removed. Pumped and gravity mixer showers may need a new mains-pressure valve.
  • Taps and fittings: older fittings designed for low pressure may need replacing.
  • Electric showers: unaffected.

The parts people forget

  • Gas supply: a combi needs much more gas than a typical system boiler while hot water is running, and the existing gas pipe is often too small. Gas work is carried out and certified by Gas Safe registered engineers.
  • Hard water: much of Sussex has hard water, and scale builds up quickly in a combi's hot-water heat exchanger. A scale reducer on the incoming supply is required in hard-water areas, and it protects your warranty.
  • System cleaning: a new boiler on dirty old pipework fails early. The system should be cleaned, filtered and dosed with inhibitor.
  • Controls: a combi in England must be fitted with an extra efficiency measure, such as weather compensation or a smart thermostat with automation and optimisation. Weather compensation is the one we recommend. See heating curves explained.

Think about a heat pump before removing the cylinder

Heat pumps heat water into a cylinder, not on demand. If a heat pump is likely in the next ten years, removing the cylinder and its cupboard now can make that switch harder and dearer. In that case, an unvented cylinder suitable for a heat pump may be the better long-term choice. See is your system heat-pump ready?

Before you decide, check

  • How many bathrooms you have, and whether two showers often run at once
  • Incoming flow rate and dynamic pressure, measured rather than guessed
  • Where the boiler will go, and how far it is from the bathroom
  • What type of showers you have and whether they suit mains pressure
  • Whether the gas supply needs upgrading
  • Whether a heat pump is likely in your plans
