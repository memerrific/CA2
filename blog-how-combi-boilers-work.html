How it works

How a combi boiler works

A combi does two jobs with one burner: it heats your radiators, and it heats mains water on demand for your taps and shower. Understanding how it switches between the two explains most of how it behaves day to day.

Two circuits, one burner

Inside a combi are two separate water circuits that never mix:

  • The heating circuit: a sealed loop of water that flows through the boiler and your radiators.
  • The hot-water circuit: fresh mains water that passes through the boiler on its way to your tap.

A diverter valve decides where the boiler's heat goes. Hot water always takes priority, so the heating pauses while a tap is running. You rarely notice, because radiators hold their warmth for a few minutes.

Heating mode

When the thermostat calls for heat, the burner lights and warms the water in the main heat exchanger. The pump circulates it round the radiators and back. The heating circuit is sealed and runs at about 1 to 1.5 bar when cold. An expansion vessel absorbs the water's expansion as it warms, and a pressure relief valve lets water out safely if the pressure ever climbs too high, usually at 3 bar.

Hot-water mode

Open a hot tap and a flow sensor detects water moving. The diverter valve switches, and hot water from the heating side passes through a small plate heat exchanger. Mains water flows through the other side of the plates and is heated on its way to the tap. Close the tap and the boiler switches back to heating.

This is why there is a short wait for hot water, and why flow is limited by the boiler's size and the mains supply. See converting to a combi for what that means in practice.

Why modern boilers condense

Burning gas produces water vapour. A condensing boiler uses an extra-large heat exchanger to cool its exhaust until that vapour turns back into water, recovering heat that older boilers sent up the flue. It only condenses when the water returning from the radiators is cool, below roughly 55 °C. Run the radiators too hot and the boiler stops condensing and loses efficiency. That's why weather compensation and lower flow temperatures matter even with a gas boiler.

The water it produces drains away through the condensate pipe. If that pipe runs outside and freezes in a cold snap, the boiler locks out. Correct sizing and insulation prevent it.

Modulation

A modern boiler can turn its burner down, as well as on and off. Combis are often sized for hot water, which needs far more power than heating a home. If the boiler can't turn down far enough, it switches on and off repeatedly on the heating side, which wastes energy and wears parts. Good setup, and choosing a boiler with a wide modulation range, keeps it running steadily.

What common problems usually mean

  • Pressure keeps dropping: a leak somewhere on the system, a failing expansion vessel or a weeping relief valve.
  • Hot radiators while a tap is running: often a sticking diverter valve.
  • Hot water goes hot and cold: often scale in the plate heat exchanger, common in hard-water areas, or a flow sensor fault.
  • Locks out in freezing weather: very often a frozen condensate pipe.
